Where Will Nature Find Refuge?

Mapping Potential Climate Refugia by 2050

Where will relatively favourable climatic conditions persist for natural ecosystems?

A climate refugium is a place where the climate changes slowly enough—or where terrain offers enough cooler, moister niches nearby—that plants and animals can stay put or move only a short distance. This map combines three signals into one index: how fast climate zones move across the land, how far warming pushes beyond normal year-to-year variability, and how much topographic shelter the landscape offers.

Method & data
  1. Baseline climate. ERA-Interim 2 m air-temperature climatology, downscaled to 10′ (~18 km) with ETOPO5 relief and a 6.5 °C/km lapse rate.
  2. Projected warming. CMIP6 multi-model median change in land temperature, 2041–2060 vs 1995–2014, for each of the 46 IPCC AR6 land regions (IPCC WGI Atlas; 32–34 models), smoothed across region borders.
  3. Climate velocity = warming rate ÷ local spatial temperature gradient (km/yr), after Loarie et al. 2009. Low velocity = an organism needs to move only a little to keep the same temperature.
  4. Climate novelty = warming ÷ interannual variability (σ of detrended 1965–2014 annual means across models). Low values mean the future stays closer to familiar conditions.
  5. Topographic diversity = elevation range within ~55 km (ETOPO5), a proxy for micro-refugia.
  6. Refugia index = 0.5 × (low velocity) + 0.3 × (low novelty) + 0.2 × (topographic diversity), each converted to a 0–1 percentile using the SSP2-4.5 distribution. Classes are SSP2-4.5 quintiles applied unchanged to all scenarios, so the scenarios are directly comparable.

This is a screening index, not a prediction for individual species. Velocities depend on grid resolution (finer grids give lower values) and precipitation, fire and land use are not included. Permanent ice and land south of 60° S are excluded.

Method & data
  1. Species richness. Number of species whose range overlaps each 0.5° cell: amphibians and mammals (IUCN Red List range maps), birds (BirdLife International), reptiles (GARD, Roll et al. 2017), rasterised by the rasterSp project. Threatened = IUCN categories VU, EN, CR (amphibians, birds, mammals).
  2. Refugia. Map 01 index averaged to 0.5°.
  3. Bivariate classes. Terciles of richness (all species-bearing land) × terciles of the refugia index (SSP2-4.5 thresholds, kept fixed for other scenarios).
  4. Hotspots. The 35 biodiversity hotspots of Conservation International (Mittermeier et al. 2004, plus Forests of East Australia added in 2011); hotspot statistics use the 10′ refugia grid.

Range maps overestimate where species actually occur at fine scales, and richness treats every species equally. Use it to compare regions, not to plan individual reserves.
